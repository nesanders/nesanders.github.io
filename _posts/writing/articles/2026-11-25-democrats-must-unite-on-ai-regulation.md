*This article was written with Bruce Schneier and originally published by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/nov/25/democrats-ai-regulation-plan) on 2026-11-25.*

*Voters are demanding action on the issue. We need to make AI work for the American public, not the tech oligarchs.*

This month’s elections demonstrated the American public’s urgent demand for change. But they also revealed a glaring problem for the Democrats’ policy agenda: they haven’t presented a compelling plan around AI.

This is important, because AI isn’t just a political issue in itself: it’s also a primary force behind both the [economic disruption](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/06/ai-disruption-job-quitting) and [political corruption](https://futurism.com/future-society/ai-billionaires-elections-backfiring) that drove so many voters to the polls.

As AI companies and their boosters tossed around [hundreds](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2026/oct/31/elon-musk-midterms-spending-visualized) [of millions](https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2026/11/06/ai-big-tech-2026-midterms-anthropic-openai-01152312) of dollars in the 2026 elections, and the Trump administration [lined up](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/tech-companies-danger-trump) unequivocally alongside industry oligarchs, Democratic leaders have so far done more [dodging](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/23/2028-democrats-ai-bernie-sanders) than leading on the issue. Despite the massive overall shift towards the Democratic party, voters emerged from the election [split](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/exit-poll-trump-midterms-voters-economy-disapproval-rcna607045) on which party would best help control this technology.

Yet the public has been quite clear that they want government to take action. As an October [survey](https://www.reuters.com/world/most-us-voters-say-trump-congress-dont-take-ai-risks-seriously-reutersipsos-poll-2026-10-07/) shows, 84% of voters say AI threatens American workers, and 57% say the Trump administration has not taken AI risks seriously enough or at all. Eighty percent of Democrats and 61% of Republicans want stricter AI regulation. This is one of the rare, emotional issues that [crosses](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/01/us/politics/liberals-conservatives-data-centers.html) party lines.

Some politicians have channeled that anger. The Democratic senators-elect Sherrod Brown in Ohio, James Talarico in Texas, Troy Jackson in Maine, and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan all ran – and won – against [giveaways](https://time.com/article/2026/11/04/midterm-elections-results-ai-industry-data-centers/) to tech billionaires and the general undercurrent of backlash against AI.

Among other proposals, the Vermont senator Bernie Sanders has advocated that the government [acquire](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/press-releases/news-sanders-introduces-legislation-to-create-7-trillion-ai-sovereign-wealth-fund/) shares of AI companies, the California representative Ro Khanna has called to give [workers](https://fedscoop.com/ro-khanna-ai-wealth-distribution-company-boards/) a say in AI use in their workplaces, and the New York governor Kathy Hochul has signed [safeguards](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-legislation-expanding-historic-empire-ai-consortium-and-establishing) for consumers using AI companions into law.

But there are other critical aspects of AI that have not yet made it on to the public agenda, where political leaders should be putting forward powerful ideas.

These include addressing the staggering inequality driven by the growing wealth of big tech, ensuring fair and universal access to this technology as it becomes increasingly embedded in life and work, and protecting consumers and workers from the exploitative uses of AI.

To be successful in the 2028 presidential campaigns, Democrats must align around an AI policy agenda that shows they are willing to fight for the change voters are demanding.

The congressional caucus must walk into the Capitol in January prepared to take immediate legislative action on this agenda, showing they can channel the wishes of the public and set aside the self-interested demands of AI billionaires. That includes rejecting their [campaign funds](https://issueone.org/press/reeling-from-major-lawsuit-losses-big-tech-injects-huge-sums-into-influence-operations/) and barring Congress members from [stock trading](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/congressional-stock-trading-explained). And they should coordinate action across the state houses they control, as well as those they do not.

In [our book](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262049948/rewiring-democracy/) Rewiring Democracy, we studied examples from around the world of how AI will impact democratic politics. Following this month’s election, the path forward for US AI policy – and its intersections with broader economic and social policy – is clearer than ever.

We need to make AI work for the American public, not the tech oligarchs.

We propose a policy platform with three planks: curbing the power of big tech, building AI that works for everyone, and putting people in charge of where the technology is used.

The first plank is restraining the big tech companies that Trump has [cozied up](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/05/trump-power-ai-techscape) to. Today, a few trillion-dollar corporations are rigging the tech ecosystem – really, the country’s entire economy – for their own benefit, generating wealth from models trained on our knowledge, our grid, our land, and our air and water, while forcing the costs of their harmful practices onto the rest of us. We need to rein in their power and make them pay the costs of the problems they create for society.

Like so many voters, we would start with datacenters. We would match and extend [Massachusetts](https://www.mass.gov/executive-orders/no-658-establishing-requirements-for-responsible-data-center-development-and-operations-in-massachusetts-to-protect-and-support-ratepayers-communities-and-the-environment)’ policy preventing companies from foisting pollution and price spikes on datacenter host communities. We would require datacenter developers to match their power consumption with new clean energy, and install energy storage systems to compensate for their strain on the energy grid.

But we also can learn from the UK’s [experience](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2026/nov/07/lanarkshire-ai-datacentre-datavita-chapelhall-national-grid-renewable-energy), after a data enter developer secured more than £200m of public guarantees for a project before backtracking on promises to use renewable energy.

The challenge is to ensure local control, while defending towns against developers with nation-state-like coffers. Congress should go beyond Massachusetts’ policy and insist that states receiving federal energy and economic grants require their cities to execute transparent, binding and enforceable agreements with datacenter developers. These agreements would need to meet a minimum standard of annual payments per megawatt-hour of energy consumption that accounts proportionately for local infrastructure impacts.

We would not stop with datacenters. Borrowing from Senator [Elizabeth Warren](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-for-time-tax-ai-and-invest-in-people/), we would seek big, structural change, particularly to the incentives that give corporations tax benefits for replacing people with machines.

We would levy an excise tax on the energy used by large AI datacenters, a simple mechanism to force those profiting from the “hyperscaling” of AI to internalize its true costs. We would direct the funds recovered from the tax to a counterbalancing reduction in payroll taxes and to support unemployment insurance, so that private investment in AI always translates to public investment in people.

There are other structural changes the party might also consider, such as the [sovereign wealth fund](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/01/opinion/artificial-intelligence-bernie-sanders.html) proposed by Sanders or the [AI dividend](https://www.statesman.com/news/politics/state/article/james-talarico-calls-ai-dividends-help-22377208.php) proposed by Talarico. We [urge](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jun/08/bernie-sanders-ai-sovereign-wealth-fund-plan) inclusion of a mechanism like the excise tax because it shifts incentives, forcing AI providers to pay all of the costs for truly advantageous uses and discouraging frivolous ones.

We would also bar companies from [consolidating](https://time.com/7322418/chat-gpt-open-ai-nvidia-tech-monopoly/) control of the full AI stack across chips, compute infrastructure, and model development and strengthen SEC [scrutiny](https://news.bloombergtax.com/financial-accounting/relationships-among-ai-companies-suppliers-draw-sec-scrutiny) of AI company’s business transactions and accounting. These steps would mitigate anti-competitive behavior and end the [circular investment](https://theconversation.com/whos-paying-whom-the-circular-money-machine-behind-the-ai-boom-or-bubble-293938) that threatens to inflate a [bubble](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/06/us-tech-stock-ai-bubble-bursting-market-meltdown-explainer) that could lead to a catastrophic market crash.

The second plank is building AI that benefits everyone, not just the barons of the industry. AI should operate like a public utility – a regulated service built and operated for the public benefit – and not an extractive product foisted upon consumers by unaccountable corporations for the benefit of their owners.

This starts with providing a public alternative to corporate AI: [public AI](https://publicai.network) models that are developed transparently under democratic oversight and control. These models must follow ethical guidelines such as compensating content creators for training data and strictly comply with regulations.

Most importantly, models developed by public agencies would be incentivized to create public good instead of private profit. Public AI would not replace for-profit AI, but provide an alternative for consumers and enterprises wary of big tech, and set a competitive baseline for corporate models.

Building AI in that mold might sound like a pipe dream, but countries such as [Switzerland](https://www.democracyrenovator.com/p/rewiring-democracy-now-switzerland) and [Spain](https://alia.gob.es/eng) are doing it already. Congress should invest in a permanent [federal AI research](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/heinrich-young-rounds-booker-reintroduce-bipartisan-legislation-to-expand-access-to-artificial-intelligence-research) program, as a bipartisan coalition including the New Mexico senator Martin Heinrich and New Jersey senator Cory Booker have proposed, to lead this effort.

Public AI should include guaranteeing public access to AI services. [South Korea](https://thenextweb.com/news/south-korea-free-ai-chatbot-all-citizens-domestic-models) is doing this at scale today, offering free universal AI access to its 52 million residents. The US could do it differently, borrowing from the history of New Deal programs such as the [Rural Electrification Act](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/econ_focus/2020/q1/economic_history), which provided federal low-interest loans to non-profit, member-owned cooperatives to build local infrastructure.

The datacenters that power US AI don’t have to be behemoths owned and operated by big tech companies to maximize shareholder value. They could be small, local facilities owned and operated cooperatively by the communities they serve to benefit their neighbors.

The third plank is giving voters and workers power over where and how AI is used.

Voters say by nearly a seven-to-one ratio that companies developing or deploying AI should be [liable](https://theaipi.org/poll-shows-voters-oppose-open-sourcing-ai-models-support-regulatory-representation-on-boards-and-say-ai-risks-outweigh-benefits-2/) for the harms of their products, and yet Congress has not established that accountability. This is yet another kind of corporate [handout](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jun/24/ai-errors-companies-responsibility): why hire human writers, lawyers or doctors when AIs are not only cheaper, but they also absolve employers whenever they make a mistake?

Here’s how it should work: Congress should curtail the power of corporate owners to use AI to exploit, and expand the power of workers to exercise control over how AI is used against them.

Congress should mandate that AI use be disclosed, where appropriate. There should be strict guardrails on the use of AI in [decision-making](https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/sen-markey-rep-clarke-reintroduce-ai-civil-rights-act-to-eliminate-ai-discrimination-and-enact-guardrails-on-use-of-algorithms-in-decisions-impacting-peoples-rights-civil-liberties-livelihoods) in contexts implicating human rights and civil liberties to prevent algorithmic discrimination. There should be clear rules surrounding AI’s use in lethal military applications and [surveillance](https://www.schneier.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Schneier-AI-and-Spying.pdf), whether by marketers or police forces.

Warren has proposed the [Accountable Capitalism Act](https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/warren-renews-fight-for-economic-growth-that-benefits-workers), which would give workers at least a 40% share of the largest corporations’ board seats so they can exercise some control over corporate decision making, including around political spending and use of AI.

We prefer this broad-based “codetermination” reform to El-Sayed’s [proposal](https://abdulforsenate.com/2026/10/ai-under-control/) aimed solely at AI companies, since most large corporations now use AI in their business operations, and it matches longstanding practice in some [European](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2021/06/29/what-does-codetermination-do/) countries such as Germany.

* * *

There are myriad details to be decided in redistributing power over how and where AI is used. We’re likely to get only some of them right and, even then, the shifting capability of the technology and circumstances of its use will cast doubt on those choices over time. That’s to be expected.

The immediate imperative is to put in place a regulatory framework for governing the use of AI that acts as a solid, federal regulatory floor that can be tweaked and built upon over time.

Since Americans don’t [trust](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/AI-Polling-Memo_4.2026.pdf) the Trump administration or tech executives to carry out this enforcement, Congress should pass legislation to create the regulatory framework. It could base legislation on the framework already adopted in [California](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/11/18/governor-newsoms-nation-leading-ai-safety-framework-is-getting-even-stronger-as-california-releases-next-steps-on-mandatory-safety-standards-and-oversight/), and plan to review it and add specificity at least annually. Part of the initial action must be [clarifying](https://ash.harvard.edu/articles/whos-accountable-for-ai-usage-in-digital-campaign-ads-right-now-no-one/) the boundaries between where each federal regulatory agency acts, and strengthening their authorities and enforcement budgets.

There are great debates to be had about each of the policy proposals we’ve put forward, and true urgency to have them. The Trump administration has made clear what the alternative path is: an administration “[100% in favor](https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/117569389226840480)” of “[super intelligence](https://time.com/article/2026/10/23/why-trump-s-rebrand-of-artificial-intelligence-to-super-intelligent-matters/)”, promoting “[self-policing](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/30/nx-s1-5985699/trump-self-police-ai-development)” by AI companies while [blocking](https://apnews.com/article/trump-artificial-intelligence-chatbots-ai-23a0e44ab05402ddfe9cdfd0bffa0ade) state efforts to regulate the technology and giving corrupt [handouts](https://www.propublica.org/article/burgum-public-land-data-centers-investments) to the corporations benefitting from it.

It’s all [justified](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c6gqdgg8w59xo) through the lens of an arms race with China, despite the administration subverting [sanctions](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/07/business/economy/nvidia-chips-chinese-firms.html) to sell chips to Chinese firms and ignoring Chinese [espionage](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/08/us/politics/rubio-nvidia-ceo-chinese-spy.html) targeting Trump’s industry allies.

Is any of this politically realistic? It seems more politically advantageous for Democrats to be caught trying than to give up without a fight.

If Trump stays true to his [promise](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/americas/us-politics/trump-midterms-defeat-congress-republicans-b3081091.html) to block Democrats’ legislative action, they should stand prepared to work with Republican members to overcome those vetoes. Republican AI defectors such as Governor [Ron DeSantis](https://www.politico.com/news/2025/12/27/we-have-to-reject-that-with-every-fiber-of-our-being-desantis-emerges-as-a-chief-ai-skeptic-00704333) and Senator [Josh Hawley](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/02/hawley-tests-trumps-hands-off-approach-to-ai-01104288) suggest this is possible.

Where Democrats cannot work with Republicans, they should jump at the opportunity to draw a contrast for voters. The party needs to go into 2028 with a positive vision, one that could lead to more and more enduring change.
